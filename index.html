01 — 1837 to 1843

Before code,
there was a card.

Charles Babbage designed a machine that could follow instructions. Ada Lovelace saw further — that the instructions themselves, not the metal, were the invention.

The Analytical Engine was never finished. But the idea survived on punched cards borrowed from the Jacquard loom: a hole meant yes, blank meant no. It was the first time a human intention was translated into something a machine could act on without a person turning every gear by hand.

02 — 1943 to 1949

The machine
gets a memory.

ENIAC filled a room. Colossus helped read German teleprinter traffic. But every new task meant rewiring by hand — programming as physical labor.

In 1945 John von Neumann’s report on the EDVAC, drawing on the ENIAC team’s work, described a machine that keeps its instructions in memory beside its data. In 1948 the Manchester Baby ran the first stored program. Binary — nothing but on and off — became the only dialect the hardware would ever natively understand.

03 — 1954 to 1970

One idea.
Many languages.

FORTRAN, COBOL, ALGOL, LISP, BASIC — each one a bet that programmers shouldn't have to think like the machine. Grace Hopper’s A-0 compiler (1952) made the bet first: let the machine do the translating. Drag the marker through history. The instruction never changes. Only how humans are asked to say it does.

06 — 2001 to 2015

Software stops
being a product.

Open source, version control, the cloud, the API, the app store. Code stopped shipping in a box and started living everywhere at once — dense, fast, and mostly invisible.

07 — 2012 to Now

The language
turns around.

For seventy years, humans learned to speak like machines. Neural networks learn the opposite way: from patterns in language itself. A word becomes a token. Tokens fall into relationships. Relationships become the shape of what comes next.
